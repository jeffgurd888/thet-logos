The Glacial Clock

Exhibit · Two cycles, geared

Two cycles, geared together. The slow cycle is a glacial clock: ice grows toward the poles, then melts back, and as it breathes it walks Earth's three moments of inertia along a fixed path. The fast cycle is the flip — Euler's equations integrated live, exactly as in The Flipping Point, but fed the clock's current moments at every step. The gear between them is one number: the gap between the polar moment C and the middle moment B. On the clock's spending half the gap closes and the flip's period stretches toward the freeze; on the restoring half the gap reopens and the flips quicken. The slow clock spends the fast cycle's timing. A switch chooses how the ice moves: SINGLE POLE MELT — one pole, asymmetric, the gap is spent and the body flips — or DUAL POLE MELT — both poles together, balanced, the gap is held and the spin stays steady. It opens on single-pole melt, already tumbling. The fences are printed on the face — read them before you read the dial: the amounts this clock moves are fenced as beyond any physical melt, and Earth's real spin is not on the seat this exhibit forces.

The moments — the clock's hands, live

In single-pole mode they follow the ring-model path (per ocean mass moved pole-ward: ΔA = ΔB = +⅜·mR², ΔC = −¾·mR², R = 6.371×10⁶ m). In dual-pole mode the equatorial pair moves as one — A ≡ B at every moment — and C rides exactly one Earth-today gap above them, so the polar gap is held. Their ranks are recomputed from the numbers themselves at every moment.

This gauge is the thesis of the piece. The flip's period is not the flip's own property — it is set by the gaps around the spin axis, and the glacial clock spends one of those gaps. Drag the gap toward zero and the analytic period stretches toward infinity — the freeze. At zero, C and B are equal, B's rank changes, and past it B stops being the intermediate axis at all: there is no longer an unstable seat for the spin to sit on. The flipping point is not a place or a date — it is the moment two moments become equal and the spin's axis changes rank. In dual-pole mode the needle never leaves the Earth-today mark: the gap is held, and there is no flip period to print — a balanced seat has no timing to spend.

Fenced at full strength: the clock's full swing moves ≈5×10²¹ kg — about 4 ocean masses, about 200× every glacier and ice sheet on Earth — pole-ward. That is beyond any physical melt. And the direction matters: real melt moves the needle the other way — melting everything raises C by +7.9×10³² kg·m² and widens this gap (the TOTAL MELT preset in The Flipping Point). The spending half of this clock is ice moving pole-ward, which no melt does; it is drawn so the gear between the two cycles can be seen.

Exhibit — live mathematics, honestly fenced · NO CLAIM

Labeled plainly: this is an exhibit — Euler's equations integrated live and a glacial drive geared into them, which is mathematics you can check, wrapped around a planet it does not threaten. It makes NO CLAIM about Earth's future. Earth's spin sits on its largest-moment axis; melting deepens that seat (see the parent exhibits). The flip is real physics — for bodies spinning on their middle axis: the space-station wingnut, and tumbling asteroids like Toutatis.

The Earth face, stated plainly: the globe is the melt-loop exhibit's own presentation — both polar caps drawn on the planet, the pole marked, the axis riding its precession cone — with The Flipping Point's axis assembly drawn through it: the red angular-momentum line, the blue figure-axis trace, and the three body axes. The flips happen in this picture. In single-pole mode the whole assembly somersaults and the marked pole swings end-over-end around the red line; in dual-pole mode the pole rides its cone, steady, the way the melt loop's Earth rides it.

The two melt modes, labeled plainly: toy paths in the parents' ring-model units, described in their qualitative terms — asymmetry between the equatorial moments destabilizes the intermediate seat; symmetric redistribution preserves the balance. Single-pole melt is the asymmetric path: the (C − B) gap is spent and the body flips. Dual-pole melt is the symmetric path: A and B stay equal, the gap is held at Earth today's guard, and the spin stays steady. Neither mode is a model of any real ice sheet, and neither says anything about Earth's future.

The forced seat, stated plainly: to make the intermediate axis's timing visible, this exhibit forces Earth's spin onto axis B. With Earth's real spin on C, the same glacial arithmetic merely breathes the stable wobble's period (the ~305-day Euler wobble at today's moments) — no flip is possible, and none is shown here for the real configuration. The seat, not the planet, is the protection. The built-in ~10° tilt is aimed along the direction the instability itself amplifies — the generic nudge. (Aimed the other way, the same tilt first dies toward the axis and the flip waits far longer: the saddle at the intermediate seat has both faces, and both are real.)

Provenance of the numbers: the moments, the ring-model path, the melt ceiling (+7.9×10³² kg·m² on C), and the ≈5×10²¹ kg boundary figure are the parent exhibits' own, reused unchanged — in The Flipping Point's verification the flip period stretched from 1,374 to 19,455 toy-time as the gap closed, and its angular momentum held to ~10⁻¹² relative drift. This page's readouts are its own live computations from the same formulas.

Rigid-body fences, stated plainly: the body in this toy is perfectly rigid and torque-free. Earth is neither — it flows, its bulge follows its spin, and the Moon and Sun hold torques on it. Every one of those differences makes the real planet more settled in its seat, not less. The drawing is a schematic Earth (its flattening exaggerated); the moments are the numbers the clock sets, and the integration is exact for the body those numbers describe.
