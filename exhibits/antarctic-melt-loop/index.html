Why Does Antarctic Polar Ice Melt Matter?

Exhibit · Melt–Precession Idea

Because the melt talks back. Earth's axis cones around once every 25,772 years; that precession paces the ice ages — and the melting ice, in return, nudges the precession's tempo and drags the pole. This is the loop, and you can steer it: drag the Earth, scrub the cycle, flip the melt between DUAL and MONO and watch what changes — then flip the new switch and ask what happens if the melt does not come back, or leaves too fast to follow.

The feedback ring · four links

Now lighting: precession → sunlight timing

Each link lights for its quarter of the cycle. Precession retimes the seasons' sunlight; the sunlight times the melt; the melt rearranges Earth's mass — its moments of inertia; and the moments are the very handle the Moon and Sun grip to drive the precession. The loop closes.

Our position · the countdowns

Our position

11,700 years into the Holocene interglacial. The natural next glacial inception sits roughly 50,000 years out — and human carbon has postponed it past 100,000. We are early in the interglacial, not late.

Countdown A — the flip in who leads

Antarctica's loss rate overtakes Greenland's: scenario window ~2040–2070, if differential acceleration persists. Assumption, printed: Antarctica's loss keeps accelerating faster than Greenland's. If it doesn't, the window moves — or never arrives.

Countdown B — the day itself

The melt's lengthening of the day overtakes tidal braking: ~2100 under high emissions, when the melt term (~+2.6 ms/century) passes the tidal term (+2.3 ms/century). Scrub this toy to a melt pulse peak and you can watch the toy's melt term cross the tidal line.

Countdown C — the threshold

West Antarctica is a threshold, not a date. Once committed, its collapse would run for centuries. No year is registered for it, and none should be read into this panel.

Exhibit of an idea — a picture, not a proof and not a forecast

Labeled plainly: this is an exhibit of an IDEA — a picture of a hypothesis, not a proof and not a forecast. The idea — glacial melt coupling back into the precession that paces it — was published in advance on 2026-10-25, dated and pre-registered with explicit kill conditions, so the data can grade it in the open: the Melt–Precession Coupling idea paper.

The fences, stated plainly: the toy's pole migration is magnified thousands of times — the real drift is about a centimeter a year, invisible at this scale, so the dot's walk is a diagram, not a measurement. The tempo shift is capped at the paper's honest ceiling: +0.3%, and that only for melting all modern ice; in MONO loop mode the toy's pulses are sized as single-basin events, so its tempo barely moves while the pole does the talking — that contrast is the lesson, and it is a toy parameter, printed here; in one-way mode the drain is the whole inventory, so the tempo climbs to the same ceiling and stays there. The countdowns are scenario arithmetic from published rates, not predictions with dates attached; the idea itself predicts no crustal displacement, no global flood layer, and no axis flip — melt deepens the stable seat the spin already sits in. One-way mode is the no-return scenario: it breaks the loop on purpose. The real glacial cycle returns the water to the caps over ~100,000 years; a melt that does not come back is the question this mode asks. Depletion pace magnified for visibility — at satellite-era rates, losing all of Antarctica takes millennia; Greenland centuries-to-millennia. Fast loss separates the axes only in the model — in the real Earth the same lag exists (the bulge relaxes over thousands of years), which is why today's pole is still chasing yesterday's ice. The Coriolis term is real physics at a small address: it steers the ocean and stirs the length of day at the sub-millisecond level; the mass term still owns the pole.
