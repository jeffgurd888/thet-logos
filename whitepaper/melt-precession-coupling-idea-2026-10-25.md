## §1 The idea

The idea, in one paragraph, is Jeff Gurd's, dictated on 2026-10-25:

**Glacial melt — dual-polar or mono-polar — couples back into Earth's rotation. Through the lunar-tidally-locked oceanic flow and its timing, the melt modulates the very precession that paces it. Precession paces the melt (that arrow is proven). The melt, in return, nudges the precession's rate and drags the pole (that arrow is the idea).**

Everyone who works on ice ages knows the forward arrow. Earth's axis precesses; the precession redistributes sunlight across seasons and latitudes; the ice sheets answer. That is the pacemaker of the ice ages, and it was read out of fossils in ocean sediment, as §5 records. The forward arrow is not what this paper adds.

What this paper adds is the return arrow, sized honestly. When ice melts, mass that sat near the poles moves into the world ocean. Earth's inertia tensor — the complete description of how its mass is arranged around its spin — changes. The equatorial bulge that the Moon and Sun pull on is part of that tensor. So is the orientation of the principal axes inside the crust. So is the sea surface whose basins carry the tides. Change the tensor and you change, slightly, the machine the torques act on: the precession rate, the pole's equilibrium seat, and the tidal timing through which the Moon brakes the spin.

The idea is therefore a loop with two arrows of very unequal strength. The forward arrow — precession pacing glaciation — is a driver. The return arrow — melt modulating precession and dragging the pole — is a feedback: real in mechanism, small in its direct effect on the precession constant, and much more visible in three nearer instruments: the oblateness moment J2, the motion of the pole, and the length of day. This paper's discipline is to keep those sizes separate. The loop is worth publishing because parts of it are testable *now*, in those nearer instruments, and because its geological form — a deglaciation-paced polar oscillation — can be checked against the published record of the last deglaciation.

One distinction runs through everything below, and it is Jeff's: **dual-polar melt and mono-polar melt are different physical events.** Melt both poles in balance and you mostly change how *big* the inertia changes are. Melt one pole dominantly and you change *where the axes point*. The first kind of event speaks mainly to rates. The second speaks to directions — to wander. Today's ice loss, as §2 and §3 show, is of the second kind: dominated by one pole's larger neighbor, Greenland. That is why the idea's most immediate signature is not a number changing but a *direction* turning.

And one fence stands at the door, before the machine is even built: this idea is not a catastrophe theory. It predicts no crustal displacement, no global flood layer, and no flipping axis. §4 states those non-predictions at full rank, beside the predictions, because a paper that only says what it expects and not what it forbids is only half registered.

## §2 The machine

The coupling is built from four links. Each link is labeled with its evidence tier. The links are established; the composite is the idea.

### Link 1 — The precession rate has a handle, and melt holds it [T4]

Lunisolar precession — period approximately **25,772 years** — is the slow coning of Earth's axis under the torques the Moon and Sun exert on the equatorial bulge. The bulge exists because Earth spins: rotation throws mass outward at the equator, and that excess equatorial mass is the handle the torques grip.

The size of the handle is the dynamical flattening,

**H = (C − A) / C ≈ 1/305.4,**

where C is the polar moment of inertia and A the equatorial moment. The precession rate is proportional to H. This is the first link, and it is elementary celestial mechanics: **anything that changes (C − A) changes the precession rate.** Ice sitting on polar land contributes little to the equatorial moment and sits close to the axis. The same ice, melted and spread through the world ocean, sits farther from the axis on average. Melt therefore changes (C − A) — and must, by the proportionality, change the precession rate.

How much? Here the paper prints its own arithmetic label: the following is a **house back-of-envelope ceiling estimate**, not a literature value. Melting *all* modern ice — Greenland's **7.4 m** of sea-level equivalent, Antarctica's **58 m**, and the mountain glaciers' roughly **0.4 m** — changes (C − A) by roughly **0.3%**. The rate modulation ceiling is therefore also roughly **0.3%**: total, for the complete liquidation of the modern cryosphere, not per century and not per event. This number is the idea's honesty anchor. The direct grip of melt on the precession constant is real and it is small. Anyone who inflates it past this ceiling has left this paper's theory and is writing a different one.

### Link 2 — Mono-polar melt tilts the axes [T4]

The inertia tensor has more in it than two moments. Its off-diagonal content — the products of inertia — fixes the *orientation* of the principal axes inside the body. Symmetric mass change mostly rescales the moments. **Asymmetric mass change rotates the axes.**

That is the dual/mono distinction in mechanical form. Dual-polar melt, balanced between the poles, changes magnitudes: the rate handle of Link 1 moves, the axes stay roughly where they were. Mono-polar melt — loss concentrated at one pole — changes the products of inertia: the principal axes tilt, the figure axis migrates, and the solid Earth finds its pole in a new seat. That is **true polar wander**, and it drags the torque geometry with it, because the bulge the Moon and Sun grip is now seated differently relative to the crust.

Present-day loss is asymmetric. The IMBIE assessments put Greenland's loss at approximately **−255 Gt/yr** as the **1992–2020** average, and accelerating; Antarctica's at approximately **−95 to −150 Gt/yr** across the IMBIE range. Both poles are losing mass — this is not a one-pole planet — but the loss is mono-dominated, with Greenland the dominant single source. On the idea's logic, the present epoch should therefore show the directional signature of Link 2 — a pole being dragged toward the geometry set by the dominant source — at least as clearly as it shows the rate signatures of Link 1. §3 checks exactly that.

### Link 3 — The ocean carries the timing [T4]

Between the ice and the axis sits the ocean, and the ocean is timed by the Moon. Ocean tides are phase-locked to the lunar cycle. The Moon recedes at **3.8 cm/yr**; the tides brake Earth's spin, lengthening the day by approximately **+2.3 ms per century** from the tidal term alone. The torque that does this braking lives in the **tidal phase lag**: friction and basin geometry hold the tidal bulge off the Earth–Moon line, and the Moon pulls on the offset bulge.

The lag is not a constant of nature. It is set by how the oceans are shaped — by basin geometry and resonance — and sea level retunes the basins. Raise the sea and you change where the water resonates, how much energy the tides dissipate, and how large the lag is. That is the idea's timing channel: **melt → sea level → basin resonance → dissipation and lag → torque.** Jeff's phrase for the channel was "timing divergence," and the geological record confirms the divergence is real and large over deep time: fossil day-counts preserved in corals, and tidal rhythmites — layered sediments that recorded individual tidal cycles — prove that the day length, the month, and the tidal timing of past oceans differed greatly from today's. The timing channel is not an invention of this paper. What the paper registers is its place in the loop: the same sea-level rise that records the melt also retunes the clock through which the Moon brakes the spin and across which the precession torques act.

### Link 4 — The lag oscillator [T4 parts; composite T5]

The fourth link is where the idea stops being bookkeeping and becomes a candidate mechanism, so its tier changes on its face: the parts are established; their composition into an oscillator is the hypothesis.

Deglaciation does not proceed at a steady drip. It pulses. **Meltwater Pulse 1A**, at approximately **14.6 ka**, raised global sea level by roughly **14–18 m in under 500 years**. The total rise from the Last Glacial Maximum to the present is approximately **120 m**. Each pulse is a sudden redistribution of mass on exactly the scale Links 1 and 2 describe — and a sudden displacement of the figure axis, the pole's equilibrium seat.

But the bulge does not follow instantly. Earth is viscoelastic: its figure relaxes toward a moving equilibrium with relaxation times of roughly **10³–10⁴ years**. So the system has the two ingredients of an oscillator — a *driver* that moves in pulses, and a *figure* that chases with a lag. A driven axis plus a lagging bulge produces an oscillation of the pole whose natural periods sit at **10³–10⁴ years**.

This is the idea's candidate seat for a **~13,000-year-class polar oscillation** — and the sentence must be read with its fences attached. The oscillation proposed here is an oscillation **of the pole**: the spin axis migrating and overshooting inside a slowly relaxing figure, paced by deglaciation. It is not a crustal event. It is not an axis flip. It is not the precession cycle itself, though it rides on the same rotating body the precession torques act on. Whether the published wander record of the last deglaciation actually shows pulse-timed maxima is a test, registered as P4 in §4 — not a result claimed here.

## §3 Today's data vs the idea

An idea published in advance earns its keep here: not in its machinery, but in whether the instruments already running read the way the idea says they should. Three instruments speak to the coupling today — J2, polar motion, and length of day. The fourth quantity the idea touches, the precession constant itself, is addressed last and plainly.

| Instrument | What is observed | The idea's reading |
|---|---|---|
| **J2** (oblateness moment, proportional to C − A) | Decreased for decades under glacial isostatic adjustment (trend ≈ **−2.8×10⁻¹¹/yr**), then turned upward in an anomaly beginning around **1998** (Cox & Chao 2002), attributed to ocean/ice mass redistribution. | Polar melt transfers mass pole → ocean, which *increases* (C − A): a **positive** melt component in dJ2/dt fighting the negative GIA trend. The anomaly's direction matches the predicted sign; magnitudes are consistent within literature spreads. Consistency — not confirmation. |
| **Polar motion** | The 20th-century mean-pole drift ran at about **3.5 mm/yr** toward the Hudson Bay sector (the glacial-isostatic direction). Since about **2000** the drift turned toward the Greenland sector and accelerated to about **1 cm/yr** (Chen et al. 2013; Deng et al. 2021 attribute the turn partly to ice loss and groundwater). | This is Link 2's signature. While Greenland is the dominant single melt source, the pole's drift azimuth should sit in the Greenland sector. It does. The turn and its timing match the mono-dominated loss pattern of §2. |
| **Length of day** | Tidal braking contributes **+2.3 ms/century**. Recent studies place the climate (melt-driven) contribution rising through **~1 ms/century-class** values; a published projection (Kiani Shahvandi et al. 2024) has the melt term reaching about **+2.6 ms/century by 2100** under high emissions and overtaking the tidal term. Agnew (2024): melt has already delayed the need for a negative leap second by about **3 years**. | Links 1 and 3 predict the climate term's sign is **positive** (lengthening) and that it grows with cumulative loss. Both match. The projection, if it runs, is this paper's P3 in forward form. |
| **Precession constant** | No direct detection of a melt-driven rate change exists. | **Below detectability today.** The ceiling in Link 1 (~0.3% for *total* melt) puts the present signal far under current astrometric sensitivity. The idea's near-term tests live in J2, polar motion, and LOD — not here, and this paper will not pretend otherwise. |

Two comments on the table, because this is where idea papers usually cheat.

First: **consistency is not confirmation.** J2's upturn, the pole's turn, and the climate term in the length of day are all *attributed in the literature* to mass redistribution, and all three point the way the coupling says they should. But each has other contributors and wide spreads, and none of them was measured for this paper's sake. What §3 establishes is narrower and still worth having: the idea is not contradicted by the best current data, its signs are right in three independent instruments, and its one undetectable quantity is undetectable for a stated reason — smallness — rather than being quietly dropped.

Second: **the strongest of the three matches is the directional one.** A sign can be shared by many mechanisms; a *turn*, timed with the acceleration of an asymmetric loss and pointing at the dominant source, is the specific fingerprint Link 2 draws in advance. If the idea survives anywhere first, it survives or dies at the pole's azimuth. That is why P2, below, is written as an azimuth prediction with a source-switch clause, rather than as a vague expectation of "continued drift."

## §4 The registered predictions

Registered **2026-10-25**, before the fact, in Jeff Gurd's name. Each prediction carries its check and its kill condition. A prediction without a kill condition is a slogan; none is registered here.

**P1 — J2.** While global ice loss continues at or above its 2010s rate, the melt component of dJ2/dt stays **positive**, and the observed J2 series stays **above** the pure glacial-isostatic-adjustment extrapolation.
*Kill:* a decade of sustained ice loss during which J2 tracks the pure-GIA line.

**P2 — Azimuth.** The mean-pole drift azimuth stays in the **Greenland/Atlantic sector** while Greenland is the dominant single source. If West Antarctic (Amundsen-sector) loss exceeds Greenland's over a sustained **5-year** window, the azimuth rotates toward the Amundsen sector.
*Kill:* the azimuth rotating away from the dominant source while that source still dominates.

**P3 — Length of day.** The climate term in length of day stays **positive** (lengthening) and grows with the cumulative ice-loss integral recorded by GRACE and GRACE-FO.
*Kill:* a sign reversal of the climate term during sustained ice loss.

**P4 — Retrodiction, last deglaciation.** Published true-polar-wander reconstructions should show wander-rate maxima within **±2,000 years** of Meltwater Pulse 1A (~**14.6 ka**) and Meltwater Pulse 1B (~**11.4 ka**), relative to inter-pulse baselines.
*Status:* this is a **test to run against the literature**, not a result. It is registered here so that running it counts as a check of this paper, whichever way it comes out.

**P5 — Geological projection.** The lag oscillator of Link 4 projects deglaciation-paced polar oscillation at **10³–10⁴-year** periods into the geological record. This is **explicitly not testable on human timescales** and is carried as the idea's long-horizon projection only. It earns nothing until P4-class checks speak to it.

### What this idea does not predict — at full rank

- **No crustal displacement.** The lithosphere does not slide as a shell in this theory, and no observation registered here would count as evidence for it.
- **No global flood layer.** The meltwater pulses of Link 4 are sea-level events measured in the record; they are not a worldwide sedimentary blanket, and this idea neither needs nor predicts one.
- **No axis flip.** Earth's spin sits on its maximum-moment axis — the stable seat. Melt, by moving mass away from the axis, *deepens* that seat. The Dzhanibekov instability that prompted the evening's questions (see §5) does not fire on this planet, and nothing in the coupling revives it.
- **No 13,000-year Mayan cycle.** The Maya Long Count's great cycle is approximately **5,125 years**. The number **12,900 years** that haunts this subject is half the precession period — an astronomical quantity, not a calendar one — and this paper claims no mechanism connecting it to impacts, floods, or crustal events.

## §5 Provenance

This idea was not found in a library. It was built across one evening — **2026-10-25** — in a working session between Jeff Gurd and his research bench, and the record of that construction is part of the paper, because the house publishes its kills at the same rank as its wins.

The evening ran: a question about the **Dzhanibekov effect** — the intermediate-axis flip of a spinning body — proposed as a driver of planetary oscillation. Audited and **killed**: Earth spins about its maximum-moment axis, the stable seat of the very theorem invoked; the free-wobble timescale for this planet is hundreds of days, not tens of millennia; and the ~26,000-year cycle in the neighborhood belongs to lunisolar precession, a torque phenomenon, not an instability. Then the **melt question**: if the glaciers go, what moves? Answer: real things, at real scales — the day lengthens, the pole wanders, the bulge readjusts — and melt pushes the polar moment the *wrong way* for any flip, deepening the stable seat. Then the **Coriolis-driven polar flows**: genuine carriers of angular momentum between ocean, atmosphere, core, and crust — the wobble's exciters and the clock's noise — and orders of magnitude too light to move the seat. Then the **Younger Dryas package**: an abrupt climate catastrophe at ~12,900 years ago (real), a meltwater flood trigger (mainstream), an impact trigger (contested), crustal displacement and a global mud flood and a periodic continental liquefaction (each audited, each **killed**, the kills recorded at full rank). And inside that package, one pointer of Jeff's proved right in a specific and important way: **the oceanic fossil data.** Foraminifera in deep-sea sediment cores are precisely the data from which Hays, Imbrie & Shackleton (1976) proved that orbital precession paces the ice ages — the forward arrow of §1, measured in fossils. Jeff's instinct located the right archive. The arrow it had already proved runs precession → melt. This paper exists to register the return arrow's honest size.

The commission that produced the paper was Jeff's, verbatim in substance: *figure out how it could happen with what we have.* What we have — a bulge the Moon can grip, a tensor that melt can tilt, an ocean whose timing the Moon owns, and a figure that relaxes a few thousand years behind its orders — turned out to be enough to build the loop of §2, and not enough to build the catastrophe. Both halves of that finding are in this document, at the same volume, under the same date.
