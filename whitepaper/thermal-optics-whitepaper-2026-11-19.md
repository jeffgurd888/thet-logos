## Abstract

Shoot a line of sight low across twenty miles of open water and the world does not add up the way the raw geometry says it should. The geometry is simple and this paper does it in the open: from shore to shore, the water bulges about 66⅔ feet up toward a straight shore-to-shore line, and a perfectly level beam finds the far surface fallen 267 feet below it. Yet observers low over lakes routinely see far shores and lights that those numbers say should be hidden. The resolution is not that the geometry is wrong. It is that the line of sight does not travel through nothing — it travels through a thermal gradient, and a thermal gradient is an optical element. This paper reviews that element: how temperature structure in air bends light continuously, how the whole mirage family — inferior and superior mirages, looming, sinking, stooping, towering, and the layered Fata Morgana — is one family of images written by a temperature profile, what a zoom lens does and does not do in the famous long-distance photographs, how the same over-the-horizon family appears at radio wavelengths — in the shortwave bounce and the tropospheric duct — and which controlled tests separate the air's share of the effect from the ground's. It closes with the author's motivating hypothesis, stated as his: that the thermal gradient is not a correction to the picture but a central actor in it.

## 1. The two numbers

Take a lake twenty miles across. Put a camera on one shore and a light on the other, both at the same height above the water, and ask the simplest question in surveying: what does the straight line between them pass over?

The Earth curves away under that line. The standard surveyor's rule of thumb, good to better than a percent at these distances, is that the surface falls away from a level line by about **eight inches for every mile, times the square of the distance in miles**:

> drop ≈ 8 inches × (distance in miles)²

There are two honest ways to use that rule across a lake, and they give two different numbers, because they are two different geometries.

**The tangent number: 267 feet.** Fire a beam perfectly level — tangent to the surface — from one shore. At twenty miles out, the surface has fallen 8 × 20² = 3,200 inches below the beam's line. That is 266⅔ feet, call it **267 feet**. This is the number for a beam that leaves horizontally and never bends.

**The chord number: 66⅔ feet.** Now connect the two shores directly, instrument to instrument at equal heights. The straight line between them is a chord. The water rises closest to that chord at the midpoint, ten miles out, and the bulge there is 8 × 10² = 800 inches — **66⅔ feet**. If both instruments sit lower than about 67 feet above the water, the straight chord between them runs into the lake in the middle.

Both numbers are the same curve read two ways. Neither is negotiable; bridges and tunnels surveyed across water crossings close on this geometry to centimetres.

And yet. People stand low on lakeshores with cameras and lasers, well under 67 feet, and photograph far shores, lighthouses, and whole skylines that the chord number says should be cut off at the waterline. Distant lights stay visible when the drop math says roughly 190 feet should be hidden at twenty miles from a six-foot eye height. As the author put it, in the conversation this paper grows out of:

> "that's not what is measured empirically"

That push is correct, and it deserves a precise answer rather than a dismissal. The empirical record is real — but what it measures, low over water, is not the ground alone. It measures the ground *through the air*, and the air is not empty. The rest of this paper is about what the air does.

## 2. The thermal bounce

Air is a material, and its optical density is set largely by its temperature. Warm air is thinner air; light travels slightly faster in it, and a light ray crossing a temperature gradient bends — continuously, a little at every step — toward the cooler, denser side. Where temperature changes smoothly with height, a ray's path curves smoothly with it. There are no mirrors in the sky. There is a gradient, and the gradient is a lens.

Over a lake, the gradient is strong exactly where the lake experiments are run: in the first few feet above the water. Water holds its temperature; air above it, on a calm night or a spring day when warm air slides over cold lake water, does not. A layer of cool dense air sits on the surface with warmer air above — a temperature inversion — and in that layer the refractive index of air falls off with height faster than usual. Rays travelling nearly horizontally through it curve downward, following the surface. In weak form, this is ordinary refraction, and surveyors already price it in: under standard conditions refraction bends a ray enough to cancel roughly one-seventh (about 13%) of the geometric drop — the reason the textbook "apparent drop" is smaller than the raw eight-inches rule. In strong form — an inversion steep enough that the ray's curvature matches or exceeds the curvature of the Earth itself — the ray does not escape at all. It is trapped in the layer and guided along the surface for as far as the layer lasts. That trapped state is called a **duct**.

The author has a plainer name for the whole effect, coined 2026-11-19: **the thermal bounce** — light carried over the horizon by the thermal structure of the air. His image for it, from the original conversation of 2026-10-24:

> "so the air over the lake acts as periscope"

The image is nearly exact, with one mechanical difference worth stating. A periscope bends light in two sharp bounces off mirrors. The lake bends it everywhere at once, because the air's density changes smoothly with height — less a pair of mirrors than a very long, very weak lens lying on its side, or a fibre-optic strand made of weather. Sailors have a name for the mild version, when distant ships and shores lift up into view: **looming**. In strong conditions the periscope runs the whole twenty miles, and what arrives at the eye is a genuine image of a shore that sits, geometrically, below the horizon.

One sentence from that conversation carries the whole section: **the light follows the gradient it's inside of.** That is not a metaphor. It is the mechanism.

For scale: near the surface, the refractive index of air exceeds 1 by only about 0.00029, and warming a parcel of air by one degree Celsius lowers that excess by roughly one part in a million. The bending available is therefore small per metre — but a ray spends tens of thousands of metres inside the layer, and small curvatures compound. A temperature inversion on the order of ten degrees Celsius per hundred metres of height, sustained over the path, is enough (standard atmospheric-optics textbook treatment) to bend rays at the Earth's own curvature and open a duct. Spring days on cold lakes deliver exactly that, in the first metres above the water.

## 3. The Flat Earth Hypothesis, tested

This paper exists because of a fair test, and it should say so. The conversation behind it, on 2026-10-24, was an audit of the **Flat Earth Hypothesis** — and the observations in §1 are that hypothesis's own evidence, the strongest form of it. They deserve to be stated at full strength, not caricatured:

*From a low eye over open water, distant shores, lights, and skylines are seen that the drop geometry says should be hidden. Lasers shot low across lakes arrive. Therefore — the hypothesis concludes — there is no drop, and the surface does not curve away.*

Stated that way, the hypothesis has a real foundation, and this house's rule is that every idea gets stated precisely and tested on its merits; a kill, when one comes, is a result, not a rejection. So: the observations are **genuine**. Nobody honest disputes them — §1 and §2 of this paper confirm them and explain their mechanism. What fails is the inference, and it fails in a specific, locatable place: a sighting low over water does not measure the surface. It measures the surface *through the first few feet of air above it*, and that layer — the thermal gradient of §2 — bends the line of sight down and around the bulge. The evidence is real; it is evidence about the air. The thermal bounce accounts for every one of the hypothesis's sightings without touching the geometry, because looming and ducting are not rare rescues — they are what that layer does every calm night and every cold-lake spring day.

The hypothesis is decided, as it should be, by controlled versions of its own experiments. Three tests separate the air's share from the ground's, and each one is fatal to the flat reading:

1. **Reciprocal leveling.** Put instruments on both shores and have them sight *each other simultaneously*. Whatever refraction does to one direction it does to the other, equally, so the air's contribution cancels out of the measurement. What remains is the geometric drop — and it is there, at the size the eight-inches rule predicts. This is not a laboratory curiosity; it is how water crossings are actually surveyed, and the bridges and tunnels built on those surveys close to centimetres over tens of miles. If there were no drop, that arithmetic would never close. It closes every time.

2. **The height test.** Raise the camera — with a zoom lens, as in §6 — and the hidden base of the far shore, the waterline of the far ship, comes back into view, by exactly the amount the curvature predicts for the new eye height. On a flat surface, height would change nothing about what the waterline cuts: the cut is not in the water, it is in the geometry, and height buys it back foot for predicted foot. The historical telling, from the conversation this paper is built on, runs the whole test in one move: a crew once set out to *disprove* the drop with a light shone through holes in boards, and their first runs, low over water, "proved" flatness — when they raised the whole rig to 17 feet, above the ducting layer, the same apparatus measured the drop instead. Same people, same gear, same lake; the only thing that changed was the air layer they were shooting through.

3. **The variability itself.** The far shore's waterline cut moves — up and down, day to day, with the weather. Under the hypothesis there is nothing to vary: a flat surface hides nothing, so there is nothing for a warm afternoon to give back or a cold night to take away. Under thermal optics the variability is the signature of the mechanism — the gradient strengthening and weakening, the periscope opening and closing. An effect that breathes with the temperature of the air is an effect *of* the air.

**Verdict, at full rank:** the Flat Earth Hypothesis is **TESTED and REFUTED** — refuted by the controlled versions of its own experiments. Its uncontrolled observations are confirmed, every one of them, and explained by thermal optics. That is the strongest thing this paper can say for the hypothesis, and it says it plainly: its evidence was real. Its conclusion was the air, mistaken for the ground.

## 4. The hypothetical maps, measured

**The exhibits.** The canon has three plates. **Rowbotham's zetetic diagram** (Samuel Birley Rowbotham, "Parallax," *Earth Not a Globe*, from 1849; *Zetetic Astronomy*, 1865): the disc, North Pole at the centre, sun and moon circling above — the ancestor of everything after it. **Gleason's New Standard Map of the World** (Alexander Gleason, Buffalo, 1892): the same disc professionally drafted and offered "scientifically and practically correct; as 'it is'" — with a working instrument around its rim, a red 24-hour dial under the title "Longitude and Time Calculator," patented as a time-chart (US 497,917, granted 1893). **Ferguson's Map of the Square and Stationary Earth** (Prof. Orlando Ferguson, Hot Springs, South Dakota, 1893): not a disc but a square, four angels at its four corners (Revelation 7:1), "four hundred passages in the Bible that condemn the Globe Theory" printed on the sheet. The canon does not hold one shape even internally — and the square's geometry fails on its own terms before any globe is consulted: a square's corners sit √2 ≈ 1.41 times as far from its centre as its edge midpoints, 41% farther, so its boundary has no single distance and its ice wall cannot be one circle.

**The projection underneath.** Gleason's layout is the azimuthal equidistant projection — a standard, legitimate projection of the globe, the family in the United Nations emblem — read as the territory. From its centre a point at angular distance θ is drawn at radius ρ = Rθ, so distances and bearings *from the centre* are exact. Around the parallels it stretches, by the factor θ/sin θ:

The map is exact radially and inflated tangentially, without limit at the rim. That is not Gleason's draughtsmanship failing; it is the projection's mathematics, and it prices the map's promises in advance.

## 5. The mirage family: images written by a temperature profile

The lake duct is one member of a family, and the family is best read as a single subject: **a temperature profile is an optical instrument, and a mirage is its image.** Change the sign or the shape of the gradient and the instrument changes; the physics does not.

- **Inferior mirage** — the image appears *below* the object. On a hot road or a sun-baked plain, the air at the surface is much warmer and thinner than the air above it, so rays bend *upward*, away from the surface, and light from the sky reaches the eye along a path that appears to come from the ground. The bright patch ahead on the road is sky, seen low — the eye reads it as water. The gradient here falls with height, steeply, in the first centimetres.
- **Superior mirage** — the image appears *above* the object. Over cold water or ice, an inversion puts dense air at the surface and rays bend *downward*; the image of a shore or a ship lifts into the sky above its true position. This is the lake case of §§1–3, seen from the image's side.
- **Looming, sinking, stooping, towering** — the gradient's finer handwriting. *Looming*: objects appear raised, and often nearer, because their light arrives along a downward-curved path. *Sinking*: the reverse, objects dropped toward or below the horizon. *Stooping*: the image compressed vertically. *Towering*: the image stretched vertically, shores and icebergs drawn up into cliffs and spires. Each is the same bending, differing only in how the ray curvature varies along the path and with height.
- **Fata Morgana** — the family's masterpiece, and the reason this paper carries the word *holographic* in its commission. In a strong, layered inversion — several temperature strata stacked, each bending light differently — the image of a distant shore arrives in multiple bands at once: stacked, stretched, inverted, and re-inverted strips of the same coastline, assembled into cliffs, walls, and castles that hang in the air and shift as the layers shift. It is named for the Arthurian sorceress Morgan le Fay, whose illusions were said to lure sailors in the Strait of Messina, where the effect is famous. There is no object where the castle stands. There is only a temperature profile, projecting.

The author's phrase for that last case is the **holographic mirage**, and it earns its place in this paper — with its fence stated on its face, here and wherever the phrase is used: **"holographic" is analogy, not mechanism.** A true hologram records an interference pattern in a medium and reconstructs a wavefront from it. The Fata Morgana records nothing; no interference pattern is stored anywhere, and no medium holds the image. What the phrase honestly names is the *effect on the viewer*: a structured, layered, three-dimensional-looking image, projected into open air with no screen — the gradient itself doing the projecting. Used that way, as a description of the phenomenon's appearance and not of its physics, the phrase is exact. Used any other way, it would be a costume, and this paper does not wear it.

Read as one family, the mirages stop being curiosities. They are the visible surface of a single fact: wherever air is stacked at different temperatures — which is nearly everywhere, nearly always — sight is already thermal optics, whether the observer knows it or not.

## 6. Line of sight and the zoom lens

Most modern over-the-horizon sightings arrive through a zoom lens, and the lens deserves its own honest accounting — what it does, and what it does not do.

A zoom lens does two things. It **magnifies**: it enlarges the angular size of whatever image arrives at it, so a shore that subtends almost nothing to the naked eye becomes a readable coastline of windows and waterlines. And it **resolves**: within limits set by its aperture and by the turbulence of the air itself, it separates detail the eye cannot. What it does not do is reach around geometry. A lens can only work on light that arrives. If no ray from the base of a far tower reaches the camera, no magnification manufactures one — the information is not there to enlarge. This is why the lens cuts both ways in the argument of §3: the zoom photograph of a "hidden" skyline is not the lens defeating curvature; it is the lens faithfully enlarging an image that the thermal bounce delivered. The air did the carrying. The lens did the reading.

The celebrated case is Chicago. From the Michigan shore of Lake Michigan — roughly 90 kilometres, about 56 miles, of open water — the Chicago skyline has been photographed repeatedly on strong mirage days, most famously in a widely reported series of images taken from the Michigan shore in 2015, showing the towers standing over the lake, sometimes inverted in the Fata Morgana manner. Run §1's arithmetic on that distance and the significance lands: the tangent drop at 56 miles is 8 × 56² = 25,088 inches, about **2,090 feet (637 metres)**. The tallest building in Chicago stands about 1,450 feet (442 metres). From a low camera on the Michigan shore, then, the *entire* skyline — every foot of every tower — sits below the geometric horizon. None of those photographs shows light that travelled straight. Every one of them is a picture of the gradient: an image ducted and lifted the whole way across a cold lake in spring, which is exactly when the photographs are taken. (Reported observations, T4 — the sightings are matters of public record; the arithmetic above is this paper's own.)

The zoom lens is also the instrument of the honest tests. The height test of §3 is, in practice, a zoom-lens test: camera low at the waterline, photograph the cut; camera raised a known amount, photograph again; the base that returns is the base the geometry priced. And the day-to-day variability that §3 counts against the flat reading is a zoom-lens observation too — the same lens, the same shore, the waterline cut wandering with the weather. The instrument of the mystery is the instrument of its resolution. That is usually how it goes.

## 7. The bounce across the spectrum: shortwave, tropo, and light

Light is not the only wave the sky bends home. Radio got there first, and by far wider margins, and the comparison the author asked for — the **shortwave radio bounce** set against the shorter-wave spectral **thermal bounce** that is this paper's subject — is worth making carefully, because the two rhyme almost perfectly and are, underneath, different physics. Everything in this section is established radio science, graded T4, and follows the standard radio-propagation textbook treatment, as the optics before it followed theirs.

**The shortwave bounce.** In the HF band — roughly 3 to 30 megahertz, wavelengths from a hundred metres down to ten — a signal launched upward does not necessarily leave. High above the weather, from some sixty kilometres up to several hundred, sunlight holds a layer of air partly ionised: the **ionosphere**, a plasma whose free-electron density grows with height to a peak a few hundred kilometres up. A plasma's refractive index depends on that electron density and, strongly, on the wave's own frequency — the lower the frequency, the harder it is bent. A shortwave ray climbing into denser plasma therefore bends gradually and continuously (refraction, for all the universal slang of "reflection" and "bounce") until, if its frequency is low enough for the density it meets, its path curves over and returns to Earth, hundreds to thousands of kilometres from where it left. That is **skywave**: the original over-the-horizon bounce, and the foundation of the first century of long-distance radio.

Its furniture is exact, and priced by the physics. Sent straight up, a frequency comes back only below the layer's **critical frequency** — of the order of ten megahertz for the main layer, swinging with the hour, the season, and the sunspot cycle; above it, the ray goes through to space. Sent at an angle, geometry buys more: the **maximum usable frequency** on a long path runs to roughly three times the critical frequency, and a single hop can span up to about 4,000 kilometres, with ground–sky–ground hops chaining a signal between continents. And there is a **skip zone** — a ring around the transmitter, often hundreds of kilometres wide, where the skywave cannot be heard at all: every returned ray lands beyond it, and the direct ground-hugging wave has died within the first tens of kilometres. The shortwave bounce is no gentle nudge. It is a full return, thrown over a dead zone, priced frequency by frequency; turn the dial a little and the landing place moves, or the signal is simply gone.

**Tropospheric ducting — radio's own thermal bounce.** Radio also knows this paper's effect from the inside, at wavelengths far shorter than shortwave's. FM, television, and radar live in the VHF, UHF, and microwave bands — metres down to centimetres — and by rights they are line-of-sight services that end at the horizon. But the lowest layer of the air, the troposphere, is the same air this paper has been about all along, and under an inversion it can trap these waves exactly as it traps light: when the air's refractivity falls with height steeply enough — the textbook threshold is a fall of about 157 refractivity units per kilometre, past which a ray curves with the Earth itself — the wave is guided inside a **tropospheric duct**. On those days, the "tropo openings" of the operators' language, FM stations and television channels arrive clear and strong from hundreds of kilometres away, far past their horizon, and radar paints returns from coasts and sea surfaces at ranges its geometry forbids. This is the tightest point of the whole comparison: same layer, same inversion, same neutral air, bending a wave along the surface — at wavelengths millions of times longer than light's. One difference of substance stands inside the rhyme: at radio wavelengths the refractivity leans heavily on water vapour, not on temperature alone, so the radio duct is a humidity instrument as much as a thermal one.

**The optical end.** And then there is this paper's subject, at the spectrum's far short end: visible light, wavelengths of about half a micrometre — shorter than a shortwave's by some seven to eight orders of magnitude. Nothing here is a plasma; the light ionises nothing. The index is the neutral gas's own, exceeding 1 by only about 0.00029 (§2), set by density and so by temperature, and nearly the same for every colour at once. The bending is therefore slight per metre, and it shows as the gentle continuous curve of §§2 and 5 — looming, the mirage family, the lifted shore — returning an image over the horizon only when the layer traps it outright, in the optical duct. No skip zone, no hops, no dial: the whole visible band is taken together, and the range is a lake's, not a continent's.

| | Shortwave skywave | Tropospheric ducting | The thermal bounce (optical) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wavelength** | 100–10 m (HF, 3–30 MHz) | Metres to centimetres (VHF–microwave) | About half a micrometre (visible light) |
| **The layer that does the work** | The ionosphere, a plasma tens to hundreds of kilometres up | The troposphere's lowest metres to hundreds of metres | The same troposphere — its first metres over the water |
| **The index depends on** | Free-electron density — and strongly on the wave's own frequency | Air density and, heavily, water vapour | Air density, hence temperature (§2) |
| **How sharp the bend can be** | A full return to Earth: hops of up to about 4,000 km, thrown over a skip zone | Trapped and guided along the surface for hundreds of kilometres | A gentle continuous curve; trapping only inside a duct, over tens of kilometres |
| **"Over the horizon" means** | A signal lands on another continent, having jumped the dead zone | A station, or a radar return, arrives from far past its line of sight | A shore, a ship, a skyline is *seen* past the geometric horizon |
| **The observer's instrument** | A receiver, tuned by frequency | A receiver, a television, a radar screen | The eye — and the zoom lens of §6 |

**The rhyme.** In every band, §2's sentence holds, with one word widened: *the wave follows the gradient it's inside of.* A stratified layer stands in the path; how its index changes with height decides what passes through, what turns, and what comes back; and an observer beyond the horizon receives what straight-line geometry said should never arrive. Shortwave, tropo, and the mirage are one family of over-the-horizon phenomena spread across the spectrum, and the resemblance is not a metaphor — each is refraction in a layered medium, read at a different wavelength.

**The break.** The mechanisms are different physics, and the join is graded accordingly. The ionosphere bends by plasma dispersion: free charges, an index set jointly by electron density and frequency, strong enough to throw a signal between continents and fussy enough to vanish when the dial or the hour changes. The troposphere bends by neutral-gas density: no free charges, a contrast of parts in ten thousand, nearly indifferent to colour, and strong enough only to lean an image across a lake. Nothing measured in one band may be carried into another — the skip zone teaches nothing about the mirage, and the critical frequency prices nothing about the duct over the water. The shared shape is a **correspondence — this paper's standing grade for a join — not a derivation, and not a unified theory of atmospheric propagation.**

What survives the comparison is the spine of the paper, one more time: in every band the actor is a gradient — a place where the medium changes with height — deciding what passes and what turns. At radio wavelengths, operators learned to work the gradient with dials and schedules. At the wavelength of sight it works on us unasked, every calm night over cold water — which is where this paper came in.
